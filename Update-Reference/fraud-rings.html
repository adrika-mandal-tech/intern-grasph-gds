Six hidden rings inside card payments

On paper these are separate payments by different card holders. Connect each payment to the device and the network it came from, and of them fall into six groups that share something they should not.

2 What stands out

Almost nothing is shared. Six things are crowded.

Method: count the connections on each device and network block

A device normally carries one person's payments. Counting how many payments touch each device and each network block separates the ordinary from the crowded at a glance.

Network blockDeviceEverything else
This is the measure usually called degree. It says where to look. It does not hand over the people gathered around each crowded point as a group; the next step does.
3 The rings

Follow the connections and the payments sort themselves into groups

Method: connected groups, then look-alikes and the path between two cards

Start at any payment and walk to everything it is linked to. Whatever can be reached is one group. Most payments reach nothing but their own device and network. Six groups are different. Select one to open it.

Groups were computed over payments, devices and network blocks only. Lone payments each have their own device and network block; those are not drawn, to keep the picture readable. The look-alike score (shared things divided by all things, known as Jaccard) links exactly the card pairs inside the rings ( of possible pairs) and nothing else, so on this data it is a way to explore the rings, not a separate finding. Methods that split a large group into sub-groups were tried and add nothing here: each ring has a single centre, so there is no sub-group inside it to find.
4 Is it luck?

The rings were found without the fraud mark. The mark agrees completely.

Method: compare against the mark, then test against chance

Most payments in this data are marked fraud, so any group would be mostly fraud. The question is whether a group this large could be entirely fraud by accident.

7 Going deeper

What the same approach shows on a deeper network

This data holds one kind of pattern: many identities around a single shared point. Networks with more layers hold more, and the same toolbox reaches them.

Splitting one large tangle

When thousands of parties are loosely joined through something common, such as a shared public network, community detection separates the tightly knit sub-groups inside it.

Finding the go-between

Where two groups are joined only through one party, that party is a broker. Scoring who sits on the routes between groups surfaces intermediaries that no single record reveals.

Spreading what is already known

Start from a few confirmed cases and let suspicion flow outward along the connections. Whoever sits closest to the known cases rises to the top of the review queue.

None of these three is demonstrated on this page, because this dataset has no structure for them to find: the rings do not touch each other, so there is no tangle to split and no go-between, and spreading from known cases drains into shop categories that everything links to. Showing them needs a deeper practice dataset, which would take about a day to build and check, and whose patterns would be ones we placed there ourselves.
i Method and limits

How this page was made

Source and checks

The graph was copied from the demo server on 1 October 2026 and exported in full ( things, connections). Ring membership, fraud counts and dollar totals were computed twice, once from the graph export and once directly from the source table; the page is not produced unless both agree. The chance check and the shop check were each run twice with different random starting points, and each was first tried on a planted case it had to detect.

What this page does not claim

It does not claim the data is real, that 81% fraud is realistic, or that any single shop is implicated. It does not claim the analysis ran inside the database. It shows that connecting records through what they share finds organised groups that row-by-row review does not, and that the groups hold up against an independent mark.
