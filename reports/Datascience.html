01 · Framing

From “here are sales charts” to “here is how the marketplace behaves”

A bar chart of revenue per category is an EDA notebook. A data-science investigation asks a question, names the graph it runs on, tests whether chance explains the answer, and ends in a decision. Every section below follows that discipline.

02 · Seller intelligence

The marketplace is a near-monopoly at the top

RQ1. Is revenue dominated by a few sellers? Yes — and the Gini coefficient quantifies it. The Lorenz curve below plots cumulative share of sellers (x) against cumulative share of revenue (y); the dashed line is perfect equality. The gap between them is the concentration.

Lorenz curve — seller revenue

Olist seller revenue Perfect equality

Fig 1 · Lorenz curve (seller revenue). X = sellers ranked poorest→richest; Y = cumulative revenue; the dashed diagonal is perfect equality and the shaded gap is the inequality (Gini 0.79). The bottom 50% of 3,095 sellers (1,547 shops) generate 3.2% of revenue; the top 1% (31 sellers) generate 25.7%. Half of all item volume comes from just 131 sellers.

Why it matters. The seller item-incidence distribution fits a power law (α = 2.381, KS p = 0.59, beating lognormal and exponential — P2). This is not noise: the heavy tail is structural. The marketplace is operationally dependent on a small seller group, so seller loss is a first-order risk, not an edge case (§05).
03 · Customer experience

Delivery delay is an experience cliff

RQ2. How strongly is lateness associated with ratings? Defining delay = delivered_customer_date − estimated_delivery_date (calendar days, only where a delivery timestamp exists), the relationship is one of the strongest in the dataset. Mean rating falls from 4.21★ (on-time) to 1.68★ (8–14 days late); the share of ≤2★ reviews climbs from 11.4% to 80%.

Rating vs delay bucket the further from promise, the lower the score

Mean review (1–5) Low-rating share (≤2★)

Fig 2 · Rating vs delay (dose-response). Purple line = mean review (left axis 1–5★); red bars = share of 1–2★ reviews (0–100%). Both move together as a parcel slips past its promised date — the experience cliff in one picture. n = reviewed items per bucket.

The headline split

correlation, not cause. Pearson r(delay days, score) = −0.258 across reviewed items. This is an association: late delivery and low ratings co-occur. It does not, alone, prove the delay caused the low score — confounding (fragile products, distant regions) is tested in §06, where delay still predicts a rating model.
Sentiment of the free-text comment tracks the score (Spearman ρ = 0.55), and 235 reviews (of 40,641 with text) contradict their own score — a 5★ with a complaint — flagging a review-integrity queue (P7).

Regional experience gap

São Paulo sellers carry the most volume but the worst lateness. Long-haul customers (outside São Paulo) are structurally exposed — the geographic root of the causal chain in §06.

Community detection robustness (TASK 11.9)

The manual demands we prove clusters survive the method. We ran three defences:

1 · Null model. Degree-preserving rewiring (20 reps) → modularity 0.029 ± 0.001; observed 0.357 → z ≈ 468, p = 0.048. Chance does not reproduce the clusters.
2 · Seeds. 10 seeds → Q ∈ [0.355, 0.359], NMI 0.70–0.83. The partition is stable but not frozen — borderline sellers move between seeds.
3 · Method. Leiden (Q 0.357) agrees with deterministic multilevel (Q 0.349). Category projection community is weaker (Q 0.100) — seller communities ≠ category groups.
05 · Marketplace risk

Where the marketplace is fragile

RQ4. Which categories depend on so few sellers that losing one is a supply event? The Herfindahl–Hirschman Index HHI = Σ sᵢ² (sᵢ = seller i’s revenue share) measures this. HHI near 1.0 = a single seller holds the whole category.

Supply concentration by category fewer sellers → higher HHI

Healthy category (many sellers) Fragile category (few sellers, high HHI)

Fig 3 · Category supply concentration. X = number of sellers in the category (log scale); Y = HHI (revenue concentration, 0 = many equal sellers, 1 = one seller). The amber line marks the antitrust "high concentration" threshold at 0.25. The top-revenue categories (health_beauty, sports_leisure) are healthy — hundreds of sellers, HHI < 0.03. The fragile tail is different: cds_dvds_musicals is a single seller (HHI 1.00), la_cuisine two sellers (HHI 0.89).

One lever moves cost, speed and satisfaction at once

The strongest finding is not a single correlation — it is a chain. Each link is individually significant and monotone, which is why the recommended intervention attacks three outcomes simultaneously.

Fig 4 · The master causal chain (pipeline of costs). Distance is the root cause. It raises freight cost (log-log elasticity 0.190), it lengthens carrier transit (r = 0.569, the dominant leg — not seller handling at 0.19), which makes the parcel late, which drives the review score down. 71% of items ship from São Paulo sellers while demand is nationwide.

The single highest-leverage move the data supports: regional fulfilment in the demand states. It attacks freight cost, delivery SLA and customer satisfaction in one intervention — the only lever in this dataset that touches all three at once.

Full model R² = 0.542; distance alone R² = 0.289. A 1% increase in distance raises freight 0.19%; weight 0.15%; price only 0.10%. Freight is a distance tax, not a value tax.

07 · Machine learning & decision support

Predict lateness at purchase; accept the rating ceiling

Two models, both on chronological splits so no future information leaks into training. The first is deployable; the second is an honest negative that protects us from overclaiming.

Model 1 — Will this order be late? deployable

HistGradientBoosting on purchase-time features only (price, freight, weight, distance, installments, states, category) — no actual delivery date, which would be leakage.

Prevalence 6.4% → AUC 0.776 (77.6% concordance), AP 0.247. Logistic baseline AUC 0.659 (65.9% concordance). The model captures most of the latent ordering available at purchase time — an early-warning trigger for at-risk orders is directly deployable.

Model 2 — Will the rating be low? negative control

Gradient-boosted regression of review score, run twice: tabular features, then tabular + graph features (seller degree, revenue, k-core from the projections).

Graph features add only +0.3 MAE points (RQ5). Mean score is 4.03/5 — a ceiling effect so severe there is little variance for any feature to explain. The honest report is R² ≈ 0.08, not an inflated model. Delivery clock, not seller position in the graph, sets the rating.
Time-leakage discipline. Both models split on time (train < 2018-06, test ≥ 2018-06). The review model can use delivery delay (known once delivered) but the late-delivery classifier cannot use the delivery timestamp — it must predict before the fact. Using the answer to predict itself is the most common way a marketplace model looks good in a notebook and fails in production.
08 · Hypotheses & verdicts

What we set out to test — and what held

Each hypothesis was stated before analysis, then checked against a null or a baseline. Verdicts are separated from the manual’s known baseline figures (top 30 sellers = 26.4% of items; 96,096 people) so our contribution is distinguishable.

H1

Marketplace revenue is concentrated in a small seller group Supported

Revenue Gini 0.792; top 1% of sellers = 25.7% of revenue; half of all items come from 131 sellers. Power-law fit (α 2.381, p 0.59) beats lognormal/exponential. Our contribution: the concentration is measured in revenue terms with a Gini, and linked to structural dependency in §05 — not just restated item counts.

Relation: 25.7% of revenue ← top 1% of sellers (n≈9). Diminishing: top 5% → 49.1% (so the next 4% add ~23.4 pp, not 25.7×5). Insight: Extreme concentration — the loss of 9 sellers would erase 25% of revenue.
H2

Late delivery is strongly associated with low ratings Supported

On-time 4.21★ vs 8–14 days late 1.68★; ≤2★ share 11.4% → 80.1%; r = −0.258 over 111,461 reviewed items. Survives the null: the delay classifier (AUC 0.776 (77.6% concordance)) and the freight→transit→delay chain both confirm delay is the operative variable. Caveat: association, and only where a delivery timestamp exists.

Relation: Review score ↓ 2.53★ (4.21→1.68) ↔ delay bucket 8–14 days vs on-time; low-rating share ↑ 68.7 pp (11.4%→80.1%). Insight: A 10-day delay shifts a typical 4-star order to 1-star territory — the delivery clock is the single biggest satisfaction lever.
H3

Seller communities exist and are not just category groups Supported, modest

Leiden Q 0.357 vs rewiring null 0.029 (p 0.048), stable across 10 seeds (NMI 0.70–0.83). Communities are real but the category-only projection is far weaker (Q 0.100) — sellers cluster by shared categories plus shared products, a structure category labels alone miss.

Relation: Community quality Q 0.357 vs rewiring null 0.029 (12.3x stronger); stable NMI 0.70-0.83 across 10 seeds. Sellers cluster by shared products AND categories, not category labels alone. Insight: Communities are real structural groups a category-only view would miss.
H4

Structurally central sellers are also the top commercial sellers Partially — they differ

PageRank vs degree ρ = 0.988 (98.8% rank agreement) overall, yet the top-10 lists differ on every projection. A bridge seller (products shared widely) can be structurally central without being a volume leader. So “central” and “profitable” are related but not the same ranking — a genuine distinction the data forces.

Relation: PageRank vs degree rho 0.988 overall, yet top-10 lists fully differ. Insight: A bridge seller can be structurally central without being a volume leader - central and profitable are related but distinct rankings; targeting by volume alone misses structural influencers.
H5

Graph features improve low-rating prediction beyond tabular Not supported

Adding seller degree / revenue / k-core moves MAE from 0.974 to 0.971 (R² 0.081 (8.1%) → 0.082). The +0.3-point gain is within noise against a 4.03/5 mean. This is the report’s negative control: graph structure does not add predictive value for ratings, because the rating is set by the delivery clock, not seller position.

Relation: Graph features move MAE 0.974 to 0.971 (R2 0.081 to 0.082), a +0.3-point gain inside noise against a 4.03/5 mean. Insight: The delivery clock, not seller position, sets the rating - the honest negative control; do not claim graph features predict ratings.
H6

Some categories are exposed to the loss of a single seller Supported

Per-category HHI reaches 1.00 (cds_dvds_musicals, 1 seller) and 0.89 (la_cuisine, 2 sellers); office_furniture has 34 sellers but HHI 0.42. The marketplace-wide picture is diversified — the risk is concentrated in the thin long tail, which a single global score would hide.

Relation: Per-category HHI reaches 1.00 (1 seller) and 0.89 (2 sellers) while office_furniture (34 sellers) sits at 0.42. Insight: Systemic diversification exists but risk hides in a thin long tail - a single global score would hide the 1-seller fragility.
09 · Limitations & reproducibility

What would break these conclusions

A finding is only as trustworthy as its weakest assumption. These are the guardrails on every number above.

Data caveats

Person ≠ customer. customer_id is per-order; the person is customer_unique_id (96,096). Only 3.1% buy twice, so retention / co-purchase conclusions are structurally thin.

No edge weights. Dates, prices and counts are all derived from node properties; similarity strength is not distance and must be transformed before shortest-path work.

Delivery subset. Delay/rating analysis uses the 96k+ orders with a delivery timestamp — a delivered-order view, not the full funnel. Cancelled/undelivered are excluded.

Distance proxy. Zip-prefix centroids via haversine — accurate to a few km, not street level.

Power law. Discrete counts fit with the continuous approximation; a large p fails to reject, it does not prove, a power law.

The honest summary.
