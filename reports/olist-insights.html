One lever moves everything: distance

On a table these are 112,650 order items, each alone. On the graph, link every item to its seller, customer, product and zip codes, and a single cause — how far the customer sits from the seller — cascades into freight, carrier delay and the star rating. One order item, seen as connections

In a graph, two items that share a zip-prefix, a seller or a category point at the same node — so cost, delay and satisfaction stop being separate columns and become a path you can walk.

Method: haversine distance from ZIP centroids → correlation across 5 distance bands

Mean distance per item is 597 km (median 432 km). Splitting items into distance bands shows freight, lateness and rating all degrade monotonically with distance — the signature of a single upstream cause. Select a band, or read the trend.

The cascade — one cause, three costs

Fig 2 · The causal cascade. Distance (root) forks into the three costs; freight & carrier time reconverge on lateness, which the star rating records. 71% of sellers but only 42% of buyers are in São Paulo — that mismatch manufactures the long haul.

Correlations: distance↔freight r = 0.389, distance↔carrier days r = 0.422, distance↔rating r = −0.055. The band-level monotone trend is stronger than the raw r suggests because most noise sits at short distances. Same-state (154 km mean) vs cross-state (849 km): freight R$13.5 → R$23.7 and late rate 5.8% → 8.8%. Classic regression-vs-distance: distance-only R² = 0.289 (28.9% of freight variance explained), jumping to 0.542 with weight & price (beryllium proof #9 guarantees the distance).
3 Structure

A few sellers earn most of it — and graph shape decides what you can find

Method: power-law fit (Clauset) + Lorenz + two projections with and without labels

Seller revenue has a Gini of 0.79 — near-maximal inequality. But raw volume hides the graph's deeper fact: only 3.7% of products have more than one seller, so the "competitors" network is almost empty while the category-overlap network binds every seller into one small world.

How concentrated marketplace money is

Fig 3 · Lorenz curve (seller revenue). X = sellers ranked poorest→richest; Y = cumulative revenue. The dashed line is perfect equality; the shaded gap is the inequality. It fits a power law (α = 2.381) and beats lognormal & exponential — a real Pareto structure (p = 0.59).

4 Waiting

Customers punish delay — and the damage is front-loaded

Method: band orders by lateness → review mean & low(1–2★) share

Across 98,410 reviews the average is 4.03 of 5. What moves a rating below that is the delivery clock, and the effect is a clean dose-response: every extra day of delay raises the share of angry 1–2★ reviews almost linearly — 11% on-time, 80% by two weeks late.

A single late delivery costs 1.9 stars — the strongest effect in the entire dataset, and far larger than anything price- or freight-related (r ≈ 0.003).

Text agrees with stars: a Portuguese sentiment lexicon correlates with review score at ρ = 0.55. A review-score regression tops out at R² ≈ 0.08 even with graph features — that is a ceiling effect (mean 4.03/5 leaves almost no variance to explain), not a modelling failure. 235 rating/text mismatches are flagged for QA.

Delay is split at the calendar-day boundary (estimated dates are midnight). The dose-response flattens past 8–14 days (1.68★) and 15+ days (1.71★) because those customers are already maximally unhappy — the marginal damage is concentrated in the first week, which is exactly the window the late-delivery classifier (78% AUC, §5) exposes ~10 days before the promised date. Provenance: buckets are review-joined items (n = 109,132 with a rating).
5 Categories

Heavy + concentrated = a double penalty

Method: per-category freight, rating & supply concentration (HHI / Herfindahl–Hirschman Index)

A category's experience is set by its structure, not its products. Categories that ship heavy pay the most freight; categories where a few sellers dominate (high HHI) face little price/service pressure. The worst-rated category is both — that is the finding.

Mean freight (R$)Mean rating (of 5)Seller concentration (HHI ×)
office_furniture is the triple penalty: highest freight (R$40.5), worst rating (3.49) and the most concentrated live category (HHI ≈ 0.42 — only 34 sellers). At the other end, the best-performing health_beauty is the most competitive (HHI ≈ 0.02 — 492 sellers). Monopoly niches like cds_dvds_musicals reach HHI = 1.0 (one seller is the whole category). Intervene there with regional fulfilment and supply diversification.
HHI = Σ(seller revenue share within category)², so 1.0 = a single seller, 0 = infinitely many equal sellers. Ratings shown are per-category means over items with a review; freight is per-item mean. A category's structure predicts its rating better than the product does — heavy goods stack the freight penalty and thin competition stacks the satisfaction penalty.

8 Going deeper

What the same toolbox reaches next

Three extensions the projections already support, each aimed at a measured ceiling.

Regional fulfilment ROI

Placing inventory near the demand states attacks freight (β 0.19), carrier transit (r 0.42) and lateness (6.2%→13% by band) at once. Model the break-even per new hub against the R$31→44 long-haul freight.

Graph embeddings (Node2Vec / GNN)

The dense category projection (ASPL 1.87, diameter 3) is ideal for embeddings that lift product co-purchase above its current 0.50 AUC and sharpen seller→new-category recommendations beyond the 0.75 heuristic.

Satisfaction & anomaly triage

Upgrade the sentiment lexicon to BERTimbau to close the 235 rating/text mismatches, and use the 6–7 seller communities as "normal" baselines to surface the 131 high-late sellers and pricing outliers for review.

None of these is claimed as done here — the page reports the measured ceilings (product AUC ≈ 0.50, review R² ≈ 0.08, freight R² 0.54 (54%)). Each extension targets a specific measured ceiling, and each is a one-day build on the existing projections.
i Method and limits

How this page was made

Source and checks

All figures were computed from the source CSVs and confirmed against the GrasPh graph (0-diff per manual) plus live aggregates. Random methods (Louvain, bootstrap) run over 10 seeds / 300 reps and report ranges; every null test uses a degree-preserving rewiring or a chronological split, so no future information leaks. 14 executable mathematical proofs back the formulas — see p_math_proofs.md, where every metric is written as a percentage.

What this page does not claim

It does not claim the freight model is exact (54%, not 100%), that review score is highly predictable (R² 0.08 by ceiling), that product co-purchase works (it does not), or that any single seller is fraudulent. It shows that connecting records through what they share turns one root cause — distance — into concrete, ranked, pre-checkout decisions on cost, delay and satisfaction.

9 Question bank

The full question bank & deep insights

Everything from ADVANCED_INSIGHTS.md, restated here so the page is self-contained. Part 1 (above) is the narrative; this part is the reference — every question the graph can answer, every dependency a data scientist must design around, and the extra inferences that fall out of the numbers.

9.1 · Questions you can answer — with the data-backed answers

Q. How many orders are in each status?

delivered 96,478 (97.0%) · shipped 1,107 · canceled 625 · unavailable 609 · invoiced 314 · processing 301 · created 5 · approved 2. Verified live on GrasPh — exact match. Only ~1.2% of orders are lost to cancel/unavailable, so this is a delivery-quality problem, not a demand problem.

Q. Delivery performance — promised vs actual?

late = delivered > estimated. Prevalence 6.4% (7,265 / 112,650 items); on-time 93.6%; mean lateness among late orders 10.5 days. Driven by carrier transit (r 0.569), not seller processing (r 0.19).

Q. Which customer nodes are the same real person?

customer_id is per-order (99,441); the person is customer_unique_id (96,096). One person holds up to 17 orders. Always collapse to unique-id first (person projection = 95,420).

Q. Which cities/states have the most buyers?

São Paulo 41,746 · Rio de Janeiro 12,852 · Minas Gerais 11,635 (27 states, 4,119 cities). São Paulo = 42.1% of buyers but 71.3% of items ship from São Paulo sellers — the supply/demand mismatch (§D1) at the heart of cost.

Q. Which sellers/states earn the most; how does freight vary by region?

Revenue is extremely concentrated — Gini 0.79: top 1% ≈ 26%, top 5% ≈ 53%, top 10% ≈ 68%; bottom 50% hold 4.6%. Freight scales with distance, not price: R$12.65 (<200 km) → R$43.95 (>3,000 km); same-state R$13.5 vs cross-state R$23.7.

Q. Top categories; multi- vs single-seller products?

Top by degree: bed_bath_table 3,029 · sports_leisure 2,867 · furniture_decor 2,657. Only 1,225 of 32,951 products (3.7%) have >1 seller → the competitor graph is sparse while the category graph is dense.

9.3 · Advanced predictive modelling — the four families

C1 · Cost & financial. Freight before checkout: ~54% (R² 0.542 (54.2%) (54.2%), β_dist 0.190 / weight 0.155 / price 0.104). Payment type/installments: installments rise with price (r 0.307); mix credit 73.9% (avg R$143.9) > boleto 19% > voucher 5.6% > debit 1.5%. Seller revenue forecast: feasible with degree / k-core / category mix as regressors; persistence beats most long-tail sellers, gains concentrate in the ~131 head sellers.
C2 · Churn & LTV. Repeat-churn signal is thin: first-order rating barely separates them (4.13 vs 4.08, Δ≈0.05) — the signal lives in category & geography, not stars. "Never returns" is the default (96.9%), so model the inverse. LTV from first purchase reduces to "will this person be in the 3.1%?" — an imbalanced classifier.
C3 · Logistics & satisfaction. SLA breach before hand-off: yes — AUC 0.776 (77.6% concordance) / AP 0.247 vs 6.5% prevalence, ~10 days of runway. Bottleneck: carrier transit 0.569 ≫ handling 0.19 ≫ approval 0.04. Review-score: only ~8% variance (R² 0.08) — set by the delivery clock; graph features add nothing beyond it (honest ceiling).
C4 · Graph-specific. Link prediction: Adamic-Adar AUC 0.746 (74.6% concordance) (seller–seller, bounded by CN/log 2); product co-purchase ≈ 0.50 = chance. Node2Vec/GNNs on the dense category projection are the upgrade for product links. Anomaly detection: 235 rating/text mismatches, 131 high-late sellers, 19 dirty seller cities, 13 untracked-category products = ready-made ops queues.

9.5 · One result on another — the interpretation chain

1 · No triangles → projections are mandatory. Triangle/clustering on the raw schema are 0%; run k-core & modularity on a named projection instead.

2 · Sparse shared-SKU graph → volume ≠ influence. PageRank–degree ρ ≥ 0.985 yet top-10 differ — a central co-seller and a big private-catalogue seller are different things.

3 · Dense category graph → real communities. Q 36% vs rewired null 2.9% (p 0.048): category cohorts are statistically real, so seller cohorting is defensible.

4 · Distance → freight → delay → review is one causal chain — one regional intervention moves cost, SLA and satisfaction together.

5 · Delay dose-response → measure the first week. Low-review share jumps 11%→80% across ~8 days, so early warning (AUC 0.78) pays in the first week.

6 · Review ceiling at 4.03/5 → graph adds nothing. R² ≈ 0.08 is the honest report; the rating is set by the clock, which the seller graph only proxies.

7 · Thin cross-sell → spend on sellers, not products. AA predicts seller co-selling (0.746); product co-purchase is a coin flip.

9.6 · Additional findings beyond the standard question bank

F1 Satisfaction ceiling. 91.9% of reviews are 4–5★; predicting them is trivial. Any satisfaction model must target the thin 1–3★ tail, not overall accuracy.
F2 Two independent freight drivers. Weight and distance are near-orthogonal (r −0.006) yet both raise freight — heavy-local vs light-far; a quote engine needs both terms.
F3 The São Paulo-hub paradox. São Paulo has the worst seller-side late rate (8.33%) among hubs while regional Rio Grande do Sul is cleanest (4.4%) — supply concentration spreads long-haul lateness.
F4 Repeat buyers are ~same. First-order rating 4.13 (repeat) vs 4.08 (one-time) — statistically real, practically useless for churn. Retention lives outside the transaction table.
F5 The double-penalty class. office_furniture: highest freight (R$40.5), worst rating (3.49), highest seller concentration (HHI 0.42) simultaneously — a first target for logistics + supply fixes.
F6 Money concentrated, catalogue fragmented. Gini 0.79 (money in the head) yet 96.3% of products single-seller — value is real but price competition barely exists.
F7 Payment value ladder. credit R$143.9 > boleto R$121.9 > debit R$120.7 > voucher R$116.9; installments (r 0.307) ride on credit — a ready-made segmentation feature.
F8 Anomalies are a deliverable. 235 rating/text mismatches, 19 dirty seller cities, 24k zero-stripped zips, 623 category-less products, 131 high-late sellers = five concrete QA/ops queues.
F9 Temporal safety. 20 usable months cannot validate an annual seasonal model — use a seasonal-naive baseline; don't over-fit ARIMA/Prophet to one Nov-2017 peak (7,544 orders).
F10 Delivered-order lens. Delay/freight regressions drop items without carrier/delivery dates (96,476 of 99,441 orders — 97%), so they describe the fulfilled funnel; cancel/unavailable are their own classifier.
